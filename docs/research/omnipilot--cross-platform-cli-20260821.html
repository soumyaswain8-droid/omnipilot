01 — Corrections first

Two things I told you that the research contradicts

Correction 1 — the cost moat is much weaker than I claimed

The last report argued our structural advantage was zero marginal cost: competitors pay per hour, we pay electricity. That framing doesn't survive contact with current hosted-ASR pricing. Groq runs Whisper large-v3-turbo at roughly $0.04 per audio-hour. At 20 hours of meetings a month, that is $0.80 per user per month in transcription cost. Read AI charges $15–39.75 a seat. Their price is not set by ASR cost, and neither is ours. Cheap compute is not a moat — it is available to everyone.

Correction 2 — swapping the model improves Hindi, it does not solve it

I called the model swap the highest-leverage fix available. The measurement on your own audio holds — 33 characters to 481 on the same 30-second clip, a real and large gain. But "this fixes Hindi" was too strong. Whisper-small shows 86.9% WER on Hindi, and even large-v3 shows roughly 32% character error on cross-script code-switched speech against ~7% for same-script. Stock Whisper at any size is weak on genuine Hindi-English switching. Indic-tuned models — IndicWhisper, Shunya Labs — beat stock Whisper at every size. large-v3-turbo is a floor, not a finish line.

Both corrections point the same direction, and it is not the direction the last report pointed: the case for going beyond one Mac is stronger than I made it, and the case for local-only purity is weaker.

02 — The constraint that decides everything

Low-spec and Hindi are incompatible locally

Taken separately, each problem is solvable. Together they aren't.

An 8 GB Windows laptop with no discrete GPU can transcribe meetings locally — small at int8 does 13 minutes of audio in 1m42s on an i7-12700K, using under 1.5 GB. A one-hour meeting finishes as a background job in under ten minutes. That is a perfectly good product for English.

But small is the model with 86.9% WER on Hindi. The model that handles Hindi acceptably is large-v3-class, which we measured at 1642 MB resident and 9.5 seconds per call on an M1 — and on a CPU-only laptop runs at roughly 2.5× slower than real time, turning a one-hour meeting into a two-and-a-half-hour job.

Stated plainly

For English meetings, local works on modest hardware. For Hindi-English meetings, local requires hardware most of our target customers don't own. And Hindi-English is precisely the segment we identified as underserved and worth selling to. The two halves of the strategy pull against each other.

That is the whole reason a hosted tier has to exist. Not because local is bad — because the customers we want are the ones least able to run it.

Architecture D deserves a note: at $0.50 per recording-hour, 20 hours a month is $10 per user — more than the entire ₹599 price we sketched. Bots are cheap to build and expensive to run. That is the opposite of what most people assume, and it is why every bot-based competitor charges $15+ a seat.

04 — Recommendation

Run locally if the machine can. Fall back to hosted if it can't.

One product, one CLI, three execution tiers chosen automatically by probing the machine on first run. The user never picks an architecture; they get the best one their hardware supports.

Tier 1 · local Full local 16 GB+ · Apple Silicon or dGPU

Everything on-device: large-v3-turbo, local summarisation, screen OCR. Nothing leaves the machine. This is what you run today, and it stays the flagship — it is the version with the strongest privacy and compliance story.

COGS: zero. Marginal cost per meeting: electricity.

Tier 2 · hybrid Local capture, hosted transcription 4 GB+ · any OS, no GPU

Audio and screen capture stay local — they're cheap. Transcription and summarisation go to Groq. The recording never touches our servers; it goes device → ASR vendor → back. We store the transcript only if the user asks us to.

COGS: ~$0.80/user/month at 20 hours. Requires a zero-retention contract with the ASR vendor and disclosure of them as a sub-processor.

Tier 3 · English-only local Offline fallback 8 GB · no network, English

For users who cannot or will not send audio anywhere — regulated environments, air-gapped work, deliberate privacy. small int8 runs a one-hour meeting in under ten minutes on CPU alone.

Must be labelled honestly: this tier is not usable for Hindi. Offering it silently for Hindi meetings would reproduce the July failure at product scale.

Nobody ships this, which is both the opportunity and the warning

Tiered local/hosted routing is a documented pattern in AI infrastructure, but the research found no consumer meeting product doing automatic hardware-based tier selection. Meetily is local-only with bring-your-own cloud keys; Granola is cloud; Screenpipe is local. Being first is a real differentiator — but the sources also flag why it may not exist: quality mismatch between tiers is a known UX problem. A user whose laptop drops them to Tier 3 gets visibly worse notes and will blame the product, not their hardware. If we build this, the tier must be visible, explained, and upgradeable — never silent.

Two useful facts hide in that table. First, whisper.cpp's Vulkan backend gives a claimed 10–12× speedup on integrated graphics — Intel Arc, Iris Xe, AMD 680M — which turns a large class of ordinary office laptops from Tier 3 into something close to Tier 1. Worth testing early; it is the cheapest way to widen the local tier. Second, the caveat: some older Intel iGPUs lack the compute features and silently fall back to CPU, so "supports Vulkan" must be measured, not assumed.

Also note what the earlier audit found: only two macOS-specific dependencies exist in the whole 507-line pipeline — screencapture and the avfoundation input string. Everything else already runs everywhere.

The irony worth internalising

macOS is the hardest platform for system-audio capture, not the easiest. Windows WASAPI loopback has existed since Vista, needs no admin, no driver, and shows no permission prompt at all. Linux exposes a monitor source for every output device. macOS is the one platform where ffmpeg has no system-loopback input whatsoever — which is exactly why the Core Audio tap design has been sitting paused. Porting to Windows may be easier than finishing macOS.

06 — The artefact itself

What the CLI is made of, and what it costs to ship

Language

Rust. Not a close call once native dependencies are in play: it links whisper.cpp and ffmpeg through FFI cleanly, produces a ~3 MB static binary, and starts in single-digit milliseconds. Go cross-compiles more easily but the moment you need cgo for whisper.cpp that advantage evaporates. Python and Node both bundle a runtime and land at 50–150 MB with fragile native modules.

One correction to an assumption I carried: I had expected to reuse DevPilot's Rust CLI, but there is no Rust source in the DevPilot repo on disk — dp-cli, dp-gateway and devpilot-core are gone after the reinitialise commit. The toolchain is installed (1.94.1); the codebase is not there. This is a fresh build, not an extension.

That last cell matters more than the money. Since 2024, EV certificates no longer skip SmartScreen — new publishers get "Windows protected your PC" regardless of what they paid, until download reputation builds. Budget for early Windows install abandonment; it is not fixable with spend.

Channels, in order of effort-to-reach: a Homebrew tap and a Scoop bucket (both instant, no review), a curl | sh installer, and GitHub Releases. Add winget once the binary is signed and stable — it has the largest Windows reach but each update goes through manifest review, adding days.

Models are never bundled

A 1.6 GB model cannot ship inside an installer. The standard pattern — whisper.cpp's own — is first-run download to a cache directory with a progress bar, HTTP range-resume, and a published SHA-256 to verify before use. Host on Cloudflare R2 or Backblaze B2 rather than S3: 1.6 GB per install multiplied across users makes egress the dominant cost on S3-style pricing.

07 — Monetisation

Nothing in the binary is enforceable. Plan accordingly.

A user who has the binary can patch out any check, extract any embedded key, and run offline forever. License-key validation deters casual sharing and stops nothing else. The research is blunt about this, and the pattern across every successful paid CLI is identical:

Syncthing is the instructive one. It is excellent, widely used, entirely local — and monetises at essentially zero, because it kept no server-side layer to charge for. Skipping the server means skipping the revenue.

Our billable layer, therefore

Give away the local CLI. Charge for the things that genuinely require us: hosted transcription for machines that can't run it (Tier 2 — real marginal cost, easy to justify), team sync and shared meeting history, and the report layer — the navigable, sourced, exportable artefact people actually consume. That is a coherent free-tier story and an honest paid one, and it survives the binary being cracked because the value isn't in the binary.

08 — The price of leaving the device

What changes the moment audio leaves the laptop

This is the strongest argument against the hosted tier, and it needs stating precisely rather than waved away.

While audio never leaves the device and we never receive it, the argument that we aren't a data fiduciary under India's DPDP Act 2023 is strong — we are not processing third parties' personal data at all. The moment we transmit or store it, that changes, and a defined set of obligations attaches: plain-language notice at collection, valid consent, reasonable security safeguards, breach notification to the Data Protection Board and to affected people, a grievance officer with a 90-day resolution SLA, and honouring access, correction, erasure and consent-withdrawal rights. Penalties run to ₹250 crore for security-safeguard failures.

Two things make this more manageable than it first sounds. There is no data-localisation mandate — DPDP took a negative-list approach and no restricted-country list has been notified, so using US-based ASR vendors is currently permitted. And an ephemeral, zero-retention design — audio transcribed and immediately discarded, never written to our disk — materially narrows the exposure, though it does not eliminate it.

Vendor gotcha worth catching before it becomes a promise

Deepgram enrols hosted-API customers in its Model Improvement Program by default — meaning your customers' meeting audio trains their models unless you explicitly opt out. Do not let anyone write "we use a zero-retention provider" in marketing without naming the mode and confirming the configuration. Groq offers self-serve Zero Data Retention, with a stated abuse-monitoring log window of up to 30 days — better, but still a disclosable window, not literally nothing.

Every ASR vendor we wire in becomes a sub-processor: it must be disclosed to customers, covered by a DPA, and bound to flow-down security and retention commitments.

Regulatory research, not legal advice. The claim that local-only processing escapes fiduciary status is a reasonable architectural reading but was not found stated as an explicit DPDP exemption — it needs a data-protection attorney's sign-off before it appears in any marketing claim. The 72-hour breach window and a reported one-year retention floor are practitioner paraphrase, unverified against the final DPDP Rules text.

10 — Over to you

What needs deciding

  • Run R1 and R2 first? Two days of measurement against claims that are currently all vendor-stated. I'd do nothing else until these land — R1 in particular could change the entire ASR choice, and you already have the perfect test audio.
  • Is the free tier the local CLI? My read: yes. It costs us nothing to run, it is the strongest privacy story, and it makes the paid hosted tier an obvious upgrade rather than a paywall.
  • Do we accept becoming a data fiduciary? This is the genuine fork. Tier 2 is what makes the product sellable to the customers we identified — and it is also what puts us under DPDP with real obligations. Staying local-only keeps us clean and keeps us Mac-only.
  • Windows before finishing macOS? Counterintuitive, but system audio is genuinely easier there, and it is what gets the rest of the team using it.
  • Who is the first non-you user? Everything above assumes someone other than you wants to run this. That assumption is still untested, and it is cheaper to test than any line item in the plan.
